Wine Score Inflation
129,971 Wine Enthusiast reviews

Wine is rated out of 100.

The 100-point wine scale uses about eight points

In the public winemag dataset of 129,971 Wine Enthusiast reviews, no wine is published below 80, and 85.7% of them score between 84 and 92. Inside that band, price buys points at a falling rate, vintage changes almost nothing, and the ten most prolific reviewers differ by 3.1 points on average.

The question

What does 92 points mean?

A shelf tag that says 92 reads like a grade out of 100. That reading assumes the scale has a bottom half in use and that 92 sits well above the middle of it. The dataset lets both assumptions be checked.

Each of the 129,971 reviews has a score, a price, a title (which usually carries the vintage year) and the name of the taster. That is enough to ask three things: how much of the scale is used, what a point costs, and how much of a score depends on who wrote it.

The shape of the scale

A floor at 80 and a hump in the high 80s

8 pts

the width of the band that holds 85.7% of all wines.

The scores do not spread across a hundred points. They form a narrow hump that starts at a cliff. Nothing below 80 is published, which is an editorial choice by the magazine and says nothing about the wines. The mean is 88.45 and the median is 88.

This is inflation in a structural sense. The scale needs no year-on-year creep to be uninformative at the bottom, because the bottom is never used.

What money buys

Price works, on a log scale

Expensive wine does score higher. Against the logarithm of price the correlation is r = 0.61, which is strong for data this noisy. A tenfold step from about $10 to about $100 moves the average from 85.3 to 91.9, a gain of 6.6 points. The price chart (twenty equal-count price bins between $4 and $500) flattens after $100.

For a buyer, the cheap end of the curve is where each extra dollar buys the most points.

Vintage and taster

Vintage barely moves the score. The taster moves it more.

Average score by vintage, 1995 to 2016, is close to flat: a fitted slope of 0.09 points per decade. Good and bad years may exist for a given region, but they do not show up in the mean of a catalogue this broad.

Among the ten tasters with the most reviews, average scores run from 86.9 to 90.0, a spread of 3.1 points. That is about half of what a tenfold price increase buys, and more than a third of the band where most wines sit, from a change of author alone.

Limitations

One magazine, one snapshot

All of this comes from a single snapshot of one publication. The dataset has no review date, so it cannot show scores creeping upward over time, which is what most people mean by grade inflation. What it can show is the structural version: an 80 floor, a compressed band, and a stated range that is mostly unused.

Vintage is pulled from each wine's title with a regular expression. Titles without a year drop out of the vintage chart and stay in every other view. The taster comparison is a raw mean, so a taster who reviews more expensive regions will look more generous for that reason alone.

How it was built

Where the numbers come from

Data
The public winemag-data-130k-v2 Wine Enthusiast dataset, downloaded from a GitHub mirror by fetch_data.py
Sample
129,971 reviews with score, price, title and taster
Price model
Pearson correlation of points against log10 price, $4 to $500; averages at $9 to $11 and $90 to $110
Vintage
Year parsed from the title; linear fit of mean score on vintage, 1995 to 2016
Tasters
Mean score for the ten tasters with the most reviews
Built with
Python, pandas, SciPy, matplotlib
