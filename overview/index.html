Wine Score Inflation
129,971 Wine Enthusiast reviews

The 100-point wine scale uses about eight points

Of 129,971 Wine Enthusiast reviews, none is published below 80 and 85.7% land between 84 and 92. Inside that band, each doubling of price buys about two points, vintage changes almost nothing, and the ten most prolific reviewers differ by 3.1 points on average.

The question

What does 92 points mean?

A shelf tag that says 92 reads like a grade out of 100. That reading assumes the scale has a bottom half in use and that 92 sits well above the middle of it. Each review in the dataset has a score, a price, a title (which usually carries the vintage) and the taster's name, which is enough to check both assumptions.

The shape of the scale

A floor at 80 and a hump in the high 80s

8 pts

The width of the band that holds 85.7% of all wines.

The scores form a narrow hump that starts at a cliff. Nothing below 80 is published, which is the magazine's editorial choice and says nothing about the wines. The mean is 88.45 and the median is 88. A scale can be inflated in this structural sense without any year-on-year creep: the bottom four fifths simply never get printed.

What money buys

Each doubling of price buys about two points

Against the logarithm of price the correlation is r = 0.61, strong for data this noisy. On a log axis the curve over twenty equal-count price bins is close to a straight line: $10 to $20 adds 2.6 points, and $50 to about $114 adds 2.0. In dollars that is a steep diminishing return. A tenfold step from about $10 to about $100 moves the average from 85.3 to 91.9, and the first ten dollars of that step buy more points than the last fifty.

Vintage and taster

Vintage barely moves the score. The taster moves it more.

Average score by vintage, 1995 to 2016, is close to flat: a fitted slope of 0.09 points per decade. Good and bad years exist for a given region, but they don't show in the mean of a catalog this broad.

Among the ten tasters with the most reviews, average scores run from 86.9 to 90.0. That 3.1-point spread is about half of what a tenfold price increase buys, and more than a third of the band where most wines sit, from a change of author alone.

Limitations

One magazine, one snapshot

All of this comes from a single snapshot of one publication. The dataset has no review date, so it cannot show scores creeping upward over time, which is what most people mean by grade inflation. What it can show is the structural version: an 80 floor, a compressed band, and a stated range that is mostly unused.

Vintage is pulled from each wine's title with a regular expression. Titles without a year drop out of the vintage chart and stay in every other view. The taster comparison is a raw mean, so a taster who covers more expensive regions will look more generous for that reason alone. The price views leave out reviews with no price.
